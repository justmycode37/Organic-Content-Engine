## Brief

- **centralQuestion:** How can a dip in starlight reveal a planet we cannot directly see?
- **audience:** Curious viewers without an astronomy background
- **oneSentenceAnswer:** A possible transit links missing light to an orbiting object's size and timing, but similar signals need to be ruled out.
- **hook:** This tiny dip could be a planet.
- **payoff:** The dip is a clue to a hidden world. The evidence has to show what made it.
- **scope:** Explain transit inference with a generic schematic and one explicitly invented numerical example.
- **Exclusions:** No new discovery or named-planet measurement is claimed in the narration.; No inference about mass, atmosphere, habitability or life.; No invented telescope dataset or observatory image.

## Narration

This tiny dip could be a planet.

A telescope records starlight. If a planet crosses the star from our viewpoint, the measured brightness falls.

Matching dips at regular intervals can reveal its orbital period, if each orbit's transit is observed.

Depth carries another clue. In this simplified model, a one-percent dip means the planet's radius is one tenth of the star's.

But a faint pair of eclipsing stars can mimic that signal. Astronomers test these alternatives before calling it a planet.

The dip is a clue to a hidden world. The evidence has to show what made it.

## Claim ledger

### candidate-clue · inference

A repeating transit-like dip can indicate a planet but does not establish one by itself.

**Uncertainty:** A candidate interpretation, not a confirmation.

**Limits:** No real candidate is being analyzed in this example.

- kepler-false-positives, Section 1.1, printed pages 6-7: Transit-like signals require vetting against alternative explanations.

### transit-dimming · observation

An appropriately aligned planet crossing its star can cause a measured dip in starlight.

**Uncertainty:** The illustration explains the mechanism rather than reproducing those observations.

**Limits:** A crossing must occur along the observer's line of sight; most orbit orientations do not transit.

- charbonneau-transit, Abstract, two photometric transit detections: An observed example of this mechanism; the animation generalizes it.

### repeat-period · inference

Consecutive transits of the same planet reveal its orbital period.

**Uncertainty:** Assumes the events belong to one planet and no intervening transit was missed.

**Limits:** Gaps or other periodic sources can confuse the interpretation.

- seager-transit-geometry, Section 2, final condition; section 3.1 observables: Consecutive events identify the period used by the model.

### radius-example · illustrative-calculation

In an idealized uniform-star model, 1% blocked light corresponds to a planet-to-star radius ratio of 0.1.

**Uncertainty:** Exact within these assumptions; illustrative input, not an observed measurement.

**Limits:** Real fits account for limb darkening, grazing geometry and extra light.

- seager-transit-geometry, Section 3.1, equation (1); section 3.2.1, equation (6): Depth equals squared radius ratio in the specified idealization.

**Inputs:** Invented fractional flux loss: delta = 0.01 (dimensionless)

**Formula:** delta = (Rp/Rstar)^2; Rp/Rstar = sqrt(0.01) = 0.1

**Result:** Rp = 0.1 Rstar, or 10% of the stellar radius; no absolute radius is inferred.

**Assumptions:** Uniform projected stellar brightness (no limb darkening); Opaque dark planet fully within the stellar disk at mid-transit; No blended light or measurement noise

### binary-mimic · inference

A background eclipsing binary diluted by brighter target-star light can mimic a planetary transit.

**Uncertainty:** One possible alternative; the source describes several others.

**Limits:** This is a conceptual example of a contaminant, not a verdict on a particular target.

- kepler-false-positives, Section 1.1, Background Eclipsing or Transiting Object, printed page 7: The mission documents this astrophysical false-positive mechanism.
