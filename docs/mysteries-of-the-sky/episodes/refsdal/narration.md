# The Star That Kept Its Appointment

## 01 · The appointment

October thirtieth. Look inside that circle. Nothing. December eleventh. Same telescope, same patch of sky. Now there's a point of light. Here's the strange part. Astronomers had circled that spot before the point appeared. See these four lights around a galaxy? All four are images of one exploding star. So is the new dot, eight arcseconds away. Nobody predicted when the star would die. They predicted when another image of its death would reach Earth. How do you get a date out of a photograph?

## 02 · Four points in a cross

Here is the view from Hubble. Most of the bright, soft-edged objects in the middle are galaxies in a cluster. Look close to one of them and you can pick out four much smaller points of light arranged around it. When Patrick Kelly and his colleagues examined images taken on November tenth, twenty fourteen, those points stood out. In older exposures, the galaxy was there. The four points weren't. Now look past the points. There's a stretched patch of light threaded through the same region. That's part of a more distant spiral galaxy. The explosion actually happened in one of its spiral arms. The smooth-looking galaxy in the middle of the cross is much closer to us. The photograph has flattened two quite different depths onto one piece of sky.

## 03 · Unfold the sky

Let's turn that photograph sideways. Put the distant spiral on the left, the intervening cluster in the middle, and our telescope on the right. An explosion sends light out in many directions. In empty, flat space, only a tiny bundle aimed our way could reach our telescope. A bundle leaving at a different angle would miss us. But light crossing this particular stretch of the universe travels through spacetime curved by the cluster's mass. Some bundles that would have missed us can reach us after all. When one enters a telescope, the instrument records the direction it came from at the end of its journey. Trace that final direction straight back on the sky and you give the star an apparent position. Another bundle enters from another direction. Same star. Different place on the detector.

## 04 · From a ring to a cross

If you put a round lens directly between you and a point of light, with perfect alignment, there's no reason for one direction around the lens to be special. In that ideal picture the source can look like a ring. Move the source off center, or change the shape of the mass, and the symmetry breaks. Bright images can appear at separate positions. The foreground galaxy in Hubble's photograph is embedded in the pull of its whole cluster. This particular arrangement produced those four bright images around the galaxy. Don't picture the star itself stretched around the central object. If we could travel back and stand near the star, there would be one explosion in one spiral arm. The cross belongs to our view of it from here.

## 05 · A clock in the picture

There's a curious limitation to doing this experiment with an ordinary galaxy. Suppose two images show you the same spiral arm, down to a recognizable knot of stars. That tells you their light came from the same place. It doesn't tell you which image is showing you the older view. A spiral arm looks much the same from one year to the next. An exploding star, on the other hand, changes brightness over the weeks and months you spend watching it. Take enough exposures and you can follow that change. Now you have something recognizable moving through time. That's what made this little cross different from all the other distorted galaxies Hubble had photographed.

## 06 · Unequal journeys

Follow two bundles emitted at the same moment. Their journeys need not take the same time. There are two pieces to the delay. First there's geometry. Connect a source and a telescope by two different routes through the lens region. The routes can have different lengths. Then there's the gravitational part. The travel time also depends on the gravitational potential along each route. You need both pieces to calculate when the light will arrive. If you put a small laboratory beside either beam and measured its speed as it went by, you'd get the same speed of light. The delay is a difference between two entire journeys. That distinction matters. A bright image isn't automatically the first to arrive, and the longest-looking arc on a photograph doesn't tell you which journey took longer.

## 07 · The other spiral

Now zoom out from the four points. The mass of an individual galaxy was enough to make the little cross. The entire cluster also acts as a lens, on a much larger scale. It produces several images of the background spiral. Think of picking out the same harbor on two very distorted maps of a coastline. You can match the harbor using the bends and inlets around it, even if one map is stretched or flipped. Astronomers could do something similar with bright knots in the spiral arms. Once they knew where the explosion sat in one image of its host, they could work out where that patch of spiral arm should appear in another. There was a specific piece of sky worth watching, separated from the cross by about eight seconds of arc.

## 08 · Make a prediction

Predicting a position and predicting a date are different jobs. To do the second, researchers built models of the foreground cluster. They assigned mass to the galaxies and to broader concentrations of matter, then calculated where those models would put the images of distant sources. A model that put a familiar arc in the wrong part of the picture needed adjusting. A model that reproduced many observed images earned a closer look. Independent teams could agree fairly well on the next place to point Hubble and still give different arrival times. That's a feature of this experiment. You have to write down the forecast before you see the answer. One published analysis put the arrival around November twenty fifteen, give or take about a month. Now the telescope could test it.

## 09 · Watch the empty spot

Here are the observations that make the story work. At the right of the published composite are two black and white exposures of exactly the same patch of sky. The upper one was taken on October thirtieth. Follow the little circle down to the December exposure. Inside it, a faint point has appeared. That's a different part of the sky from the original four points. When Hubble examined the field on December eleventh, twenty fifteen, there was a new point there, where the models had said to look. Astronomers called this image S X. What arrived that December hadn't just happened in the host galaxy. Those photons had been traveling since the very same explosion whose other light was already in Hubble's archive. The date on a photograph tells you when the telescope received the news.

## 10 · Measuring the delay

You might be tempted to take the dates of the two discovery photographs, subtract one from the other, and call that the time delay. Try the same trick with two cameras photographing a lighthouse. If one camera missed the beginning of a flash, the first pictures with light in them won't mark equivalent moments. Astronomers instead measured the brightness in a series of exposures. Plot brightness against observing date and you get what's called a light curve. For the supernova, one copy was fainter than the other, so their curves needed a change in vertical scale as well as a shift in time. Slide the histories past each other until the rise and fall line up. The result uses the shape traced by many measurements. It doesn't depend on a single lucky exposure.

## 11 · Three hundred and seventy-six days

In the later analysis the delay between S X and the image called S one was about three hundred and seventy-six days, with an uncertainty of roughly six days in either direction. There's another result sitting on the vertical axis. S X had about three tenths the magnification of S one. So the late copy gave Hubble a weaker signal. Magnification and delay answer different questions. Imagine spreading a patch of a distant galaxy across more of your detector. That changes how much total light you collect from its image. Timing asks when a particular change in that light reaches you. Knowing the answer to one doesn't fix the answer to the other. Together, though, they give a cluster model two rather different things to get right.

## 12 · Weighing the foreground

Why would anyone care whether a model gets six days closer? Because the thing making these images is partly invisible. If you add up the starlight in a cluster, you're tracing its luminous galaxies. Gravitational lensing responds to all the mass along the line of sight, including dark matter. To see the difficulty, imagine drawing two slightly different mass maps. Move some mass from the region around one galaxy into a smoother distribution across the cluster. With suitable adjustments, the predicted positions of some images can stay surprisingly similar. But their travel times can change. The returning supernova gave researchers a measurement that a still picture couldn't supply. You could put two plausible maps beside the same photograph, then use the clock to ask which one describes this cluster better.

## 13 · A ruler made of time

There was a reason astronomers named the supernova Refsdal. In nineteen sixty-four, the Norwegian astronomer Sjur Refsdal described a way to use a lensed supernova to study cosmic expansion. Here's the idea. Looking at the photographs gives you angles. Angles alone don't give you the physical size of an arrangement. Take a triangle, double every side, and look at the angles. They're exactly the same. Light, however, takes time to cross distance. Combine a measured delay with a model for the gravitational lens, and you get information about a particular combination of distances between us, the lens, and the source. Together with redshifts, that lets you estimate the present expansion rate of the universe. A clock started by one dying star becomes part of a cosmic distance measurement.

## 14 · What the clock could tell us

In twenty twenty-three, the team combined the measured delays with eight different models of the cluster. They inferred an expansion rate of about sixty-five kilometers per second per megaparsec. A megaparsec is a little over three million light-years. The units describe how much extra recession speed goes with each extra unit of distance in the nearby expanding universe. The estimate had an uncertainty of several kilometers per second per megaparsec. Choosing the two models that best matched the observations shifted the central estimate to about sixty-seven. That difference is worth keeping in the picture. The timing was measured remarkably well. Translating the clock into a distance still depended on how well researchers understood the matter the light went past. This single explosion gave them a new way to make the measurement, with its own strengths and its own uncertainties.

## 15 · The last arrival

Go back to that first photograph. The soft glow in the middle is a foreground galaxy. The streak behind it is a faraway spiral. The four points around it are an explosion. And that small patch off to one side is where the same event still lay in our future, even after we'd seen it in our past. The most extraordinary thing in this picture may be the blank spot before the fifth image appeared. It was empty enough for the prediction to fail. Someone could put a circle on it, book time on a telescope, and find out. On a winter day in twenty fifteen, a few photons crossed the last bit of space to Hubble. They turned an empty circle on a map into a test of what the universe is made of.
