## Per-chapter ledger

### 01 · A planet nobody had seen
- Berlin search on 23 Sept 1846 used a Paris prediction, and the planet was found within an hour. Sources: MACTUTOR, NASA-175. **OK**
- "a faint, bluish point". No contemporary report of colour was found; Galle reported an 8th-magnitude star not on the chart. **FIX** (drop "bluish").
- Visuals: schematic telescope and star chart. Any blue colour is artistic and should not be shown as the discovery view.

### 02 · A new planet that misbehaved
- Herschel, 13 March 1781, at 19 New King Street, Bath, with a home-built reflector. He first reported a comet; the object showed a disk and moved. Source: Herschel, "Account of a Comet", Phil. Trans. 71 (1781) 492, https://doi.org/10.1098/rstl.1781.0056 (biblio). **OK**
- "did what they always did with a new planet". No planet had been discovered before, so there was no precedent. **FIX**
- Visuals: Herschel portrait (credited); the sky and motion are schematic.

### 03 · Tables that would not fit
- Bouvard's 1821 tables could not fit the pre-discovery sightings (Flamsteed 1690 onward) together with post-1781 observations, and he rejected the old ones. Sources: MACTUTOR, WIKI-DISC. **OK**
- "off by about two arcminutes". Sources disagree: KRAJ gives 50–100″ at discovery, and popular accounts say about 2′. **NOTE / soften** to "up to about two arcminutes".
- 2′ is about a fifteenth of the full Moon (~31′). Arithmetic **OK**.
- Visuals: schematic residual curve (illustrative shape, not plotted data unless digitised from Le Verrier/Airy).

### 17 · Measuring a dot
- A transit instrument plus a clock gives right ascension, and a graduated (mural/meridian) circle gives declination. Positions accurate to a few arcseconds by the 1840s. Correction for refraction. Standard practice. **OK**
- "drifting by two arcminutes". Follows ch. 03, so soften the same way.
- Visuals: schematic transit eyepiece with wires, a clock and a ledger.

### 04 · Every planet pulls on every other
- Perturbation theory, and the Jupiter–Saturn "great inequality" (~900-year period, explained by Laplace). **OK**
- The alternative hypotheses (bad data, modified gravity at large distance, an unseen body) were all discussed in the 1830s–40s (AIRY; Airy to Hussey, 1837). **OK**
- Visuals: schematic orbits.

### 05 · Working backwards
- "a young mathematician at the Paris Observatory". OBSPM states Le Verrier was "an assistant at the École polytechnique" and that Arago, the Observatory's director, asked him to take on the problem. He became director in 1854. **FIX**
- He assumed a distance near the Titius–Bode spacing (~36 AU) and worked by hand from 1845 to 1846, in memoirs of 10 Nov 1845, 1 Jun 1846 and 31 Aug 1846 (MACTUTOR, LEV-ARXIV). **OK**
- Visuals: Le Verrier portrait (credited); the equations are illustrative.

### 21 · Rowan · reading a tug
- Conjunction was around 1822. Before it Uranus was pulled forward (sped up); after it, held back. **OK** (Simplified but sound.)
- Visuals: schematic two-orbit animation.

### 06 · The prediction
- 31 Aug 1846 memoir: orbit, mass, heliocentric longitude 326°32′ for 1 Jan 1847, and apparent diameter. Disk "possible to distinguish" (MACTUTOR, LEV-ARXIV). **OK**
- "He even predicted its brightness". This was not confirmed in any source read; the memoir is documented for diameter and disk. **FIX** (say "its apparent size").
- "about five degrees east of" δ Capricorni: Le Verrier's letter of 18 Sept (Popular Science Monthly vol. 5 quoting it; letter text in Nature 85 (1910) 184, https://doi.org/10.1038/085184c0). **OK**, but this was in the *letter* to Galle, not the 31 Aug talk; see the reply.
- The Berlin chart was Bremiker's Hora XXI from the Berlin Academy, printed 1845 and not yet distributed. D'Arrest suggested using it (WIKI-DISC). **OK**
- Visuals: schematic star chart; the constellation lines are diagrammatic.

### 07 · One night in Berlin
- Letter received 23 Sept; searched that night with d'Arrest; found in under an hour. Position 52′ from the prediction (LEV-ARXIV; NASA-175 "only 1 degree"). Motion confirmed the next night. Galle's reply of 25 Sept: "the planet of which you indicated the position really exists" (MACTUTOR). **OK**
- Visuals: Galle portrait (credited); dome and chart comparison schematic.

### 08 · The rival in Cambridge
- Adams's solutions (1845–46) differed between versions; Challis began searching 29 July 1846 (MACTUTOR). **OK**
- "twice in early August". MACTUTOR and Britannica give 4 and 12 August; 12 Aug is not early. **FIX** ("twice in August").
- Priority: after the 1998 return of the "Neptune papers", KSS-2004 argue Adams does not merit equal credit. Le Verrier's published prediction and Galle's search made the discovery. The script's characterisation is acceptable; a sharper version is offered in the reply.
- Visuals: Adams portrait (credited); the Challis notebook is schematic unless a scan is licensed.

### 09 · Luck in the arithmetic
- Le Verrier a ≈ 36.2 AU and e ≈ 0.11, against actual a ≈ 30.1 AU and e ≈ 0.009. Period 164.8 yr (NSSDC; KRAJ). **OK**
- "his predicted position was right". It was within ~1°. **FIX** (soften).
- "That isn't a coincidence." Overstated: Peirce called it a "happy accident", and modern analyses say it was partly robust (conjunction ~1822) and partly fortunate. **FIX**
- Visuals: schematic overlay of predicted against true orbit (orbit sizes to scale from the values above).

### 22 · Rowan · why it still worked — **OK**.

### 10 · Seen before, but not noticed
- Galileo, 28 Dec 1612 and 27/28 Jan 1613; on 28 Jan he noted the "star" had apparently moved (KOWAL; WIKI-DISC). Also Lalande 1795, J. Herschel 1830 and Lamont 1845. **OK**
- Visuals: a Galileo notebook facsimile (BNCF Florence, Ms. Gal. 72; PD) or a schematic redraw.

### 11 · Seventeen days later
- Lassell found Triton on 10 Oct 1846, 17 days later (NASA-175; LASSELL). **OK**
- Retrograde orbit, so likely captured. **OK**
- "It came out at about seventeen Earth masses." Modern mass = 17.15 M⊕ (NSSDC). The first Triton-based estimates of 1847–48 were scattered, so the sentence should not imply 1846–47 astronomers got 17. **FIX** (clarify).
- Visuals: schematic orbit of Triton.

### 12 · What Voyager saw
- Flyby 25 Aug 1989; 1846→1989 = 143 years. Great Dark Spot; methane absorbs red (SMITH-89; NASA-FACTS). **OK**
- Winds ~2,000–2,100 km/h, the fastest in the solar system (NASA-FACTS). **OK**
- "a deep blue world". Voyager colour images were contrast-stretched; IRWIN-24 shows Neptune is a pale greenish-blue similar to Uranus. **FIX**
- Triton plumes: dark plumes ~8 km high, thought to be driven by nitrogen gas (SMITH-89 / Soderblom et al., Science 250 (1990) 410). **OK**, with "thought to be" preferred.
- Visuals: Voyager images (credited NASA/JPL). If the classic enhanced image is shown, consider the Irwin-corrected colour or a caption.

### 18 · Neptune now
- Ring arcs from stellar occultations in the 1980s (Hubbard et al., Nature 319 (1986) 636, biblio); Voyager confirmed the rings and the Adams-ring arcs (SMITH-89). **OK**
- JWST NIRCam, 21 Sept 2022. Neptune is not blue in the near-IR and is dark except for its high clouds; rings and dust bands are clear; 7 of 14 moons (WEBB-22). "Looks pale" is acceptable for the processed image. **NOTE**
- First orbit since discovery: 12 July 2011 heliocentric (11 July barycentric) (HST-2011). **OK**
- Visuals: JWST image (credited); the orbit return is schematic.

### 13 · The same method, a different answer
- The modern anomalous perihelion advance is ≈43″/century. Le Verrier (1859, CR 49, 379) found ≈38″; Newcomb (1882) revised it to ≈43″. **NOTE**: the script uses the modern value, which is acceptable.
- Vulcan: Lescarbault's claimed transit (March 1859, reported Dec 1859); Le Verrier named it in 1860; later claimed sightings. **OK**
- Visuals: schematic rosette of the precessing orbit (exaggerated, and must be labelled as such).

### 23 · Rowan · two kinds of anomaly — **OK**.

### 14 · Einstein's answer
- 18 Nov 1915 paper: 43″/century (EINSTEIN-15). **OK**
- "made his heart race". Pais (1982, p. 253) reports that Einstein told Fokker the result gave him heart palpitations, and he wrote to Ehrenfest of joyous excitement. **OK** ("later said" matches).
- Visuals: Einstein portrait (1921 PD photo by F. Schmutzer, Commons, if used); the curvature grid is schematic.

### 15 · The method today
- Radial velocity (e.g. 51 Peg b, Mayor & Queloz 1995) and transit-timing variations (e.g. Kepler-19c, Ballard et al. 2011). NASA Exoplanet Archive: https://exoplanetarchive.ipac.caltech.edu/ . **OK**
- Planet Nine (P9) is still undetected as of Nov 2026, and the debate continues. **OK**
- Visuals: schematic wobble and TTV diagrams.

### 19 · Answer
- 23 Sept 1846; within about a degree. **OK** (the discovery night ran 23–24 Sept).
