# The Planet Found on Paper

Long-form script, about 1,970 words, about 11 minutes. Titan narrates every chapter except 21–23, which are Professor Rowan Ellery's interview inserts.

## 01 · A planet nobody had seen

How do you find a planet that nobody has ever seen? In September eighteen forty-six, an astronomer in Berlin pointed a telescope at a patch of sky chosen by a man in Paris who had never looked for it himself. Within an hour of starting the search, there it was: a faint point of light that was not on the star chart. It was Neptune, the eighth planet. It had been found with arithmetic before anyone recognized it through a telescope. The clue was a planet already known, one that would not stay where the best calculations said it should be. Welcome to Mysteries of the Sky. This is the story of the planet found on paper.

## 02 · A new planet that misbehaved

In seventeen eighty-one, William Herschel was sweeping the sky with a homemade telescope in Bath, England, when he noticed an object that looked slightly fuzzy, like a tiny disk. Over the following nights it moved against the stars. It turned out to be a planet, the first discovered since ancient times. We call it Uranus. Astronomers did what they did for every known planet. They worked out its orbit and predicted where it would be in the years ahead. And Uranus slowly wandered off the predictions.

## 03 · Tables that would not fit

By eighteen twenty-one, the French astronomer Alexis Bouvard had collected decades of positions, including old sightings where people had logged Uranus as a star without realizing what it was. He could not make one orbit fit all of them. Fit the old observations, and the new ones went wrong. Fit the new ones, and the old ones went wrong. Over the next twenty years, the gap kept growing. By the eighteen forties, Uranus was off by up to about two arcminutes. That is tiny, at most a fifteenth of the width of the full Moon. But for astronomers who could predict planets far more precisely than that, it was a glaring error.

## 17 · Measuring a dot

How do you even notice a planet drifting by an arcminute or two? In the eighteen hundreds, observatories timed stars and planets as they crossed a fine wire in a fixed telescope, called a transit instrument. A precise clock gave the moment of crossing. A graduated circle on the telescope gave the height in the sky. Together, those two numbers place an object on the sky to within a few arcseconds, dozens of times finer than Uranus's error. Each night's position went into a ledger. Astronomers compared ledgers from different observatories, and different decades, and corrected each one for the tiny bending of starlight by our own atmosphere. Uranus's problem wasn't a single bad night. It was a slow, steady disagreement that showed up in every careful record.

## 04 · Every planet pulls on every other

Why should a planet stray at all? Newton's law of gravity says every mass pulls on every other mass. The Sun dominates, so each planet follows nearly an ellipse. But the planets also tug on one another, and those small pulls bend each orbit a little. Astronomers already knew how to calculate this. Jupiter and Saturn nudge each other in a slow, predictable rhythm. When they added up the pulls of all the known planets on Uranus, it still did not fit. So there were really only a few possibilities. Maybe the observations were wrong. Maybe gravity behaved differently that far from the Sun. Or maybe something unseen was pulling on Uranus.

## 05 · Working backwards

Normally, you start with the planets and calculate how they move. Urbain Le Verrier, a young mathematician in Paris, took on the reverse problem at the urging of the Paris Observatory's director. Start with the error in Uranus's motion, and ask what kind of planet, where, would have caused it. That is far harder. The unknown planet's mass, distance, and position all feed into the answer at once, and they trade off against each other. Le Verrier made a reasonable starting assumption about its distance, based on the spacing of the known planets. Then he worked through month after month of calculation, by hand, to find the orbit that would explain Uranus best.

## 06 · The prediction

On the thirty-first of August, eighteen forty-six, Le Verrier presented his result in Paris. He put the unknown planet at a precise spot along the planets' path through the sky, near the border of Capricornus. He even predicted its apparent size, and that it should show a small disk rather than a point. French astronomers were slow to search. So Le Verrier wrote to Johann Galle at the Berlin Observatory, telling him to look about five degrees east of a star called delta Capricorni. Berlin had something few observatories had: a brand new, detailed star chart of exactly that region of sky.

## 07 · One night in Berlin

Johann Galle received the letter on the twenty-third of September. That same night, he and a student, Heinrich d'Arrest, opened the dome. Galle called out the positions of the stars he saw, and d'Arrest checked them against the chart. Within about an hour, Galle read out a star that d'Arrest could not find. It was within about one degree of Le Verrier's predicted position. The next night, it had moved slightly against the stars, just as a planet should. Galle wrote back to Paris: the planet whose position you calculated really exists.

## 08 · The rival in Cambridge

In England, a young mathematician named John Couch Adams had been working on the same problem. He reached a broadly similar answer, though his results were less widely shared, and his predicted position shifted between versions. James Challis at Cambridge began a careful search in the summer of eighteen forty-six. He actually recorded Neptune twice in August without recognizing it, because he had not yet compared his observations. The credit became a bitter argument between the two countries. Today, most historians credit Le Verrier's prediction with the discovery, and see Adams's parallel work as less complete.

## 09 · Luck in the arithmetic

There's a twist. Le Verrier assumed Neptune was farther away than it really is, and his predicted orbit was quite different from the real one. Neptune is about thirty times as far from the Sun as Earth is, and it takes about a hundred and sixty-five years to go around once. Yet in eighteen forty-six, his predicted position was within a degree. That wasn't pure luck. Uranus's strongest clues came from the decades when it passed Neptune. In those years, a planet with the wrong orbit but in roughly the right place pulls on Uranus almost the same way. The prediction was sharpest exactly where it needed to be.

The calculation didn't need the whole orbit. It needed the direction of the pull during the years when the two planets were closest. Get that right, and you point to the right part of the sky.

## 10 · Seen before, but not noticed

Neptune had in fact been seen long before. Galileo sketched it in his notebook in December sixteen twelve and January sixteen thirteen, while he was watching Jupiter's moons. He recorded it as a star, and one entry even suggests he noticed it had moved. Others logged it too. Being visible was never the problem. What was missing was a reason to look at one faint dot and ask whether it belonged there. Le Verrier's numbers supplied that reason.

## 11 · Seventeen days later

Discovery came quickly after that. Only seventeen days after Galle's observation, the English astronomer William Lassell spotted a large moon circling Neptune. It is called Triton. Triton orbits backwards, in the opposite direction to Neptune's spin, which suggests it was captured rather than born there. Even better, the moon's orbit let astronomers measure Neptune's mass directly. Today that method gives about seventeen Earth masses. That is the same law of gravity doing the work again, this time on a satellite.

## 12 · What Voyager saw

For a hundred and forty-three years, Neptune stayed a small blue disk in the telescope. Then, in August nineteen eighty-nine, the Voyager two spacecraft flew past it. Neptune turned out to be a blue world with bright white clouds and a huge dark storm, nicknamed the Great Dark Spot. Its winds reach about two thousand kilometers an hour, among the fastest measured on any planet. Its blue tint comes from methane in the atmosphere, which absorbs red light. And Triton turned out to have geysers, dark plumes thought to be driven by nitrogen gas.

## 18 · Neptune now

Neptune keeps surprising us. Astronomers found hints of rings in the nineteen eighties by watching stars flicker as Neptune passed in front of them. Voyager two confirmed faint, narrow rings, including bright clumps that should have spread out long ago. In twenty twenty-two, the James Webb Space Telescope photographed Neptune in infrared light. There the planet loses its blue, its high clouds glow, and the rings stand out sharply, along with seven of its moons. And in July twenty eleven, Neptune finished its first full orbit around the Sun since Galle saw it. One Neptunian year had passed since the night in Berlin. The planet had come back to almost the same place in the sky where the arithmetic first sent a telescope.

## 13 · The same method, a different answer

Le Verrier tried the trick again. Mercury's orbit also drifts slowly. The point where it comes closest to the Sun creeps around a little faster than all the known pulls can explain, by about forty-three arcseconds every hundred years. In eighteen fifty-nine, Le Verrier proposed another hidden planet, inside Mercury's orbit. People even gave it a name, Vulcan, and several observers claimed to have seen it crossing the Sun. Nobody could confirm it, because it wasn't there.

Same kind of evidence, completely different lesson. With Uranus, the missing piece was a planet. With Mercury, the missing piece was a better theory of gravity.

## 14 · Einstein's answer

The answer to Mercury came in nineteen fifteen. Albert Einstein's general theory of relativity describes gravity as the curving of space and time by mass. Close to the Sun, where gravity is strongest, its predictions differ slightly from Newton's. Einstein calculated the effect on Mercury's orbit and found almost exactly the missing forty-three arcseconds per century. He later said the result made his heart race. An anomaly in planetary motion, the very kind that had found Neptune, now showed that Newton's law itself needed correcting.

## 15 · The method today

The idea has never gone away. Astronomers still find unseen worlds by their pull. Many planets around other stars were first detected by the slight wobble they cause in their star's motion, or by small shifts in the timing of another planet's transits. Some astronomers today are searching the outer solar system for a possible ninth planet, inferred from the way certain distant icy bodies cluster in their orbits. That idea is still debated, and no such planet has been found. It is the same kind of reasoning, waiting for its night in Berlin.

## 19 · Answer

So, how do you find a planet nobody has seen? You watch a planet you can see, extremely carefully, for a very long time. You calculate every pull you know about, and you take the leftover error seriously. Then you work backwards to the mass that would explain it, and you tell someone with a telescope and a good star chart exactly where to look. That is how Neptune appeared on the twenty-third of September, eighteen forty-six, within about a degree of a point on paper. And the same kind of stubborn leftover error, applied to Mercury, ended up telling us that gravity itself is stranger than Newton thought.
