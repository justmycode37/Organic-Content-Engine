# The Star That Weighed a Black Hole

## 01 · How much does the black hole weigh?

How much does the black hole at the center of the Milky Way weigh? Welcome to Mysteries of the Sky. To find the answer, astronomers spent decades following a single star. Its name is S two. Every sixteen years it swings around a patch of darkness and heads back out into the crowd. From Earth, all you see is a dot changing direction. Watch it now, coming into the bend. In May twenty eighteen it was moving at roughly seventy-six hundred kilometers a second. To turn that motion into a mass, we need two things the telescope sees very differently: the shape of the star's path, and the light coming from the star itself.

## 02 · Behind the dust

Start in the constellation Sagittarius, looking in toward the middle of our own galaxy. The bright band of the Milky Way breaks up into dark lanes. Those lanes are clouds of dust. In visible light they hide much of what lies behind them, including the stars we need for this experiment. Shift the observation to longer wavelengths, into the near infrared, and much more of the starlight gets through. Zoom in again. What looked like a single patch of glow breaks into a crowd. The center is about twenty-six thousand light-years away. Even the telescopes in Chile that measured this star see it as an unresolved point. There's no little picture of its surface to follow. Its small displacement against the rest of the field is the measurement.

## 03 · Keeping the right dot

Here's a problem you can try with the pictures yourself. Pick a star in the first frame. Now let several years go by. Which dot is yours? Some of its neighbors have moved too. Two stars can pass so close together in our line of sight that their light blends into a single spot. If you measure the middle of that spot, the position you write down may belong to neither star. So astronomers register the images against a reference frame and inspect the crowded encounters. In the published orbit analysis, they identified and excluded observations where confusion with another source made the positions unreliable. Removing one dubious point matters. A few bad positions near a sharp turn could make a perfectly ordinary orbit look like evidence for new physics.

## 04 · Two small coordinates

Imagine putting tracing paper over one infrared image. Mark the center of the star. Take an exposure at another date and mark it again. The horizontal and vertical offsets are two numbers, and the observing date is a third. As the marks accumulate, you start to see an arc. Keep going long enough, and the arc bends all the way around. S two takes about sixteen years to complete one circuit. That is unusually convenient. The Sun also orbits the center of the Milky Way, but its journey lasts hundreds of millions of years. With S two, an observer who was there for the close approach in two thousand two could go back to the telescope and catch the next one in twenty eighteen. Now you can test whether the same orbit works twice.

## 05 · Why the star races

An orbit like this has a long end and a tight end. The central mass sits at a focus of the ellipse, displaced from the geometric center. Draw a line from that focus to the star. Over equal intervals of time, the line sweeps out equal areas in the simple Newtonian picture. Out at the far end the line is long. A small sweep along the track encloses plenty of area, and the star moves slowly. At the close end the line is short. To sweep out the same area in the same time, the star has to cover much more of the track. See how the dots bunch up on one side of the ellipse? They are spaced at equal times. The empty stretches between them are where the star was racing.

## 06 · A scale for the orbit

The loop on the detector gives us angles. To weigh something from an orbit we also need a physical length. A small loop nearby and a much bigger loop far away can cover the same patch of sky. So here's a second way to follow the star. Split its light by wavelength. The star's atmosphere leaves recognizable features in that spectrum. As the star moves along our line of sight, those features shift. Motion toward us shifts a feature toward shorter wavelengths. Motion away shifts it toward longer wavelengths. Measure how far a known feature moved and you can estimate that component of the velocity in kilometers per second. The image tracks motion across the sky. The spectrum gives us a velocity along the missing third direction.

## 07 · Turn the orbit sideways

Tilt a model orbit until you are looking at it almost edge on. Its image collapses into a narrow sliver. The star can be moving quite fast even while it appears to hesitate at one end of that sliver. Much of its velocity is then directed along your line of sight. Now rotate the same orbit to face you. The path looks broad and the line-of-sight velocity becomes small. Researchers use both effects together. They vary the orbit's tilt, orientation, size, and timing until one model accounts for the measured positions and the shifting spectral lines at the same dates. The fit also constrains the distance to the Galactic center. That is how a tiny angle on the sky gets tied to a ruler with physical units on it.

## 08 · Weigh the center

Once you have a physical orbit and a period, there's a surprisingly direct route to the mass. Imagine two stars completing orbits of the same size, but one finishes in half the time. It needs a stronger inward pull to make the faster turn. In the simple case where one central object dominates, halving the period while holding the size fixed means four times the mass. For S two, the characteristic size of the orbit, its semimajor axis, is around a thousand times the Earth–Sun distance. Combine that with the sixteen-year period and you get a central mass of roughly four million Suns. You could cover up the central pixel of every image and still do the calculation. The track of the star supplies the evidence.

## 09 · Inside the turn

A number for the mass is only part of the story. A whole galaxy can contain far more than four million solar masses. What makes this remarkable is how small a region has to hold so much matter. At its closest, S two comes within about one hundred and twenty times the Earth–Sun distance of the central object. Even there the star turns as though almost all the mass responsible for its orbit lies inside. The orbit gives us a boundary within which that mass is concentrated. It doesn't give us a photograph of an event horizon. To appreciate the scale, the closest passage is still roughly fourteen hundred Schwarzschild radii away. S two has plenty of room to survive the encounter and carry information about it back out again.

## 10 · Catch the redshift

That close swing offered a test with an actual deadline. Near the turn in twenty eighteen, the star was moving at about two and a half percent of the speed of light. A simple Doppler calculation tells you what part of the spectral shift to expect from motion along the line of sight. But two more effects grow important here. A moving clock runs slow relative to an observer, even when its motion is sideways. And light emitted deeper in a gravitational field is shifted to longer wavelengths when it reaches a distant observer. A spectral feature in the star's atmosphere plays the role of that clock. Compare its measured wavelength with the value expected from the ordinary orbit. The remaining shift near closest approach was large enough to detect.

## 11 · What the number means

The team reported the extra shift in a unit observers often use for spectra. Expressed as an equivalent velocity, it was about two hundred kilometers per second. That's easy to misread. The star did not suddenly acquire another two hundred kilometers per second of outward motion. You take a small fractional wavelength change and multiply it by the speed of light. That gives you a convenient size for the shift. Both gravitational redshift and the time dilation associated with the star's speed contribute to it. The twenty eighteen observation detected their combined effect. To see why the distinction matters, imagine taking a spectrum at just one instant. A slightly different ordinary velocity could mimic the extra redshift in that single measurement. Following the star through the turn lets you test how the excess grows and recedes.

## 12 · Four telescopes

Getting positions fine enough for the next test required a different piece of machinery. At Paranal in Chile, the GRAVITY instrument combines near-infrared light collected by four telescopes. Think about two of them first. A wavefront from a star reaches one telescope a tiny bit earlier than it reaches the other. Bring the beams together with the right internal delay and the waves interfere. The pattern carries information about the direction the light came from. Change the star's direction just a little and the pattern shifts. Use several telescope pairs at different orientations, and you have several measurements of that direction. The large separation between telescopes gives the instrument fine angular detail. Keeping the light paths matched closely enough to make the measurement is part of the experiment.

## 13 · An ellipse that turns

Draw a Newtonian orbit around a single point mass and put a pin in the place of closest approach. Go once around the orbit. The star comes back to the pin. In general relativity the direction of closest approach advances a little on each circuit. Draw the next ellipse over the first, then another over that. The long axis gradually swings around and the track begins to resemble a rosette. We've enlarged that rotation here so you can see it. For S two the advance per orbit is only about twelve minutes of arc, a fifth of one degree. And that's an angle in the plane of the orbit. Nobody saw a star jump a fifth of a degree across the night sky. What the telescope measured was a much tinier discrepancy in a much tinier projected track.

## 14 · Missing the old track

By twenty twenty, the GRAVITY collaboration could compare the direction of the star's path before and after its close encounter using nearly three decades of observations. Most of the detectable change in direction builds up around closest approach. The long run of older positions anchors the incoming orbit. Precise positions after the turn then tell you whether the star follows the old prediction on its way back out. For a sense of the challenge, imagine turning a ruler by a fifth of a degree. Right at the pivot the two edges nearly coincide. Follow them farther out and their separation becomes easier to see. Waiting for the star to move away from the turn helped expose the difference. The observed advance agreed with the prediction for a star orbiting a compact mass in general relativity.

## 15 · Three readings of one star

Watch one more circuit. Near the far end, the star barely seems to move. Collect enough positions there and you begin to fix the shape and timing of the orbit. At the near end the spectrum shifts especially strongly, giving you a test of how motion and gravity affect the light. Follow the outgoing leg and you can test whether the orbit itself has turned. Those are three different readings from a single unresolved star. Each asks something different of the instrument, and each can go wrong in a different way. That is what makes their agreement so interesting. At the center of the Milky Way, one patch of sky contains a mass of about four million Suns. We can measure its pull, watch its effect on the star's light, and see its mark on the next lap. All because someone kept following the same little dot.
