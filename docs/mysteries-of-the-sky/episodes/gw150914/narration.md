# The Chirp That Crossed Earth

## 01 · A second copy

This little squiggle appeared at a detector in Louisiana. Less than seven milliseconds later, another detector in Washington saw a remarkably similar one. A truck or a bump at either site could shake an instrument. But what could trace the same fast-rising pattern across both sites? First, we have to ask what they were measuring.

## 02 · Two distances

Imagine four free test masses arranged around a center. A gravitational wave passing face-on changes the distance between them. At one instant the horizontal distances grow while the vertical distances shrink. Half a cycle later, the pattern reverses. The change is tiny. In this drawing it's exaggerated so you can see which way each distance changes.

## 03 · One beam becomes two

LIGO compares two perpendicular distances. A beam splitter sends laser light along two arms, each four kilometers long. Mirrors send the light back. When the two beams meet again, their crests can reinforce or cancel. If a wave lengthens one arm and shortens the other, the returning light shifts out of step. A detector reads the change in brightness. Those changes can be calibrated into strain, the fractional change in distance.

## 04 · Seven milliseconds

Here is the published strain from both sites, filtered so the signal is easier to see. Livingston sees it first. Hanford sees it six point nine milliseconds later. Slide the Hanford trace earlier by that amount, and flip it to account for the detectors' orientations. Notice how the peaks line up. They aren't identical. Each instrument adds its own noise. A rising chirp is visible in both.

## 05 · The orbit behind the line

Two black holes in orbit lose energy to gravitational waves. They spiral inward, and as their orbit speeds up, the waves come closer together. The model predicts a rising frequency, a merger, and a short, fading vibration from the black hole left behind. That changing shape is what lets the measurement tell a story about objects that sent us no light.

## 06 · The chirp

The detectors never heard a sound. They recorded how distances changed. But their measurements can be played back as audio. This is the Livingston signal, cleaned up and shifted higher so the chirp is easier to hear.
