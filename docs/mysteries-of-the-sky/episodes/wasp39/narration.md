# The Bump Beside the Peak

## 01 · A mark in the starlight

What made the little bump beside the biggest peak in this planet’s starlight? Look at the tall feature first. That one was so clear the astronomers could pick it out almost as soon as the data came in. Now look just to its left. See that shoulder? Getting from there to an explanation took a different telescope instrument, chemists working out how an alien sky behaves, and a prediction that could have been wrong. Welcome to Mysteries of the Sky. This is the story of Wasp thirty-nine b, a world we can barely see, and a particularly revealing piece of light that never reached us.

## 02 · Four days to a year

Start with the scale of the place. Put Saturn on a set of scales and you have roughly the mass of this planet. Now puff it up until it’s wider than Jupiter. Set it so close to its star that an entire year goes by in just over four Earth days. From here, about seven hundred light years away, the star and planet run together into a point. That sounds like the end of the investigation. Except, every four days, something very useful happens to that point. For a few hours our line of sight puts the planet directly in front of the star.

## 03 · A shadow with a soft edge

Imagine watching that crossing with a detector that just counts arriving light. Before the planet moves in, the count is steady. Then the leading edge takes a bite out of the star, the count falls, and eventually it recovers. Roughly two percent of the light goes missing in this case. The planet itself accounts for most of the dip. But the planet has a soft edge. Along a very narrow strip, light skims through its atmosphere and out the other side. Some of that light makes it to the telescope. Some gets absorbed on the way. And what survives depends on its wavelength.

## 04 · A longer path through the air

There’s a way to picture why that thin strip matters. Think about looking straight up through the air over your head. Now turn toward the horizon. A ray reaching you from that direction has crossed a much longer stretch of the same layer. The light grazing this planet does something similar. Even a gas spread quite thinly can intercept a measurable amount over that long path. At wavelengths the gas absorbs strongly, the shadow reaches a little higher into the atmosphere. At wavelengths that pass through more freely, the star shines closer to the planet’s center. In effect, the planet has different apparent sizes in different kinds of light.

## 17 · Why a puffy planet helps

Now imagine repeating that observation with a much smaller planet. Draw a circle around each planet and shade in an equally thick rim of atmosphere. The bigger planet has a longer rim, so the same thickness of gas covers more starlight. Wasp thirty-nine b gets another advantage. Its outer atmosphere is hot, and gravity at this puffy planet’s visible edge is relatively weak. Gas pressure drops more gradually with height under those conditions. Its absorbing layers can be spread farther apart. Both effects make the planet a generous test case. A telescope capable of reading this shadow still has a much tougher job on a small rocky world.

## 05 · Taking the light apart

On July tenth, twenty twenty-two, the James Webb Space Telescope watched one of these crossings. Inside Webb, an instrument called a spectrograph spread the incoming infrared light across a detector. Imagine laying those detector readings side by side. Each little interval of wavelength now has its own record of the star getting dimmer and brighter again. You can compare the brightness during the transit with the brightness either side of it, in that same interval. Do that along the whole detector and a single dip becomes many slightly different dips. The important detail is their depth. How much light went missing in each one?

## 06 · Turn the dips upright

Try it with two imaginary readings. In one band, the amount blocked is two percent. In the next, it’s two point one percent. That difference is only one photon out of every thousand arriving before transit, on average. On a graph of the light reaching your telescope, the second dip goes lower. Now change what you’re plotting. Put wavelength along the bottom and the fraction blocked up the side. The deeper dip becomes the higher point. That’s the trick to reading the picture we opened with. A peak in this graph means photons have gone missing. Professor Rowan Ellery puts it this way.

## 00 · The two radii

The, um, the radius here is measured with light. Change the wavelength and you change the height where the gas becomes opaque. The planet itself hasn’t grown between those two measurements.

## 18 · Counting photons

There is a cost to splitting the light into many colors. Imagine a shower of raindrops hitting a row of cups. Count the drops in one cup for just a second and the number jumps around. Give it more time, or use a wider cup, and a steadier average begins to appear. A wavelength bin works much the same way. Make it too narrow and the count becomes noisy. Make it too wide and a little feature can blend into its neighbors. That is why astronomers sometimes group neighboring readings together. Those small vertical bars next to the points show how uncertain the individual depths are.

## 07 · The large peak

Back to the tall one. Its center sits at about four point three microns. A micron is a thousandth of a millimeter. Here the planet intercepts more starlight than it does at many of the neighboring wavelengths. And in laboratory measurements, carbon dioxide absorbs strongly at just this part of the infrared. The reason has to do with motion inside a molecule. The carbon and oxygen atoms can stretch relative to one another. Light carrying the right energy excites that vibration. Collect the contributions from huge numbers of molecules and you get a band with a recognizable shape. That’s the conspicuous feature Webb saw.

## 08 · The part that didn't fit

Now slide back down the left side of that band. Around four point zero five microns there’s another rise. Much smaller. Here is where it pays to distrust a pretty curve. Telescope detectors give you a set of measurements, each with some uncertainty. A smooth line drawn through those measurements has already involved a choice. Astronomers instead calculate what the spectrum would look like for a proposed mixture of gases and compare it to the points. A mixture can fit the tall carbon dioxide peak beautifully and still miss the little rise beside it. That leftover pattern was the interesting part.

## 21 · A run of points

One point above the model could be noise. Several neighboring bins on the same side of it? That’s the shape worth checking.

## 19 · Looking for the instrument

There’s a practical reason for starting the exposure before the planet arrives. Suppose the signal drifts slowly upward through the whole visit. If you had only the time spent in front of the star, how would you know where the original brightness level belonged? Following the star before and after the crossing gives you a baseline to compare against. You can also ask whether a suspicious variation follows the transit timing, or whether it shows up when the image moves a little on the detector. Several groups analyzed the observations with different software. That’s a way to see which features survive the decisions made on the ground.

## 09 · Another way to see it

What if the instrument had put that odd feature there? One useful check was available. Webb had examined the same neighborhood of wavelengths in two different modes. One spread the light out finely and the other covered a wide range at once. Their detectors and processing choices didn’t provide identical tests, but the extra absorption appeared in both sets of results. Now another problem came into focus. A chemical model based on what settles into balance at the planet’s local temperature and pressure gave too little of a promising candidate. Something seemed to be missing from the account of how this atmosphere works.

## 10 · What the star supplies

Think of a parcel of rising gas. Down deep on this hydrogen-rich world, some of the sulfur travels in a molecule called hydrogen sulfide. Up higher, conditions change. Ultraviolet light from the star can break up water molecules, producing highly reactive fragments. Those fragments help strip hydrogen from the sulfur-bearing gas. Sulfur can then combine with oxygen, through a series of reactions. The key ingredient that the earlier calculation left out was a continuing supply of energy from above. The star was doing two jobs. Some of its light passed through the air and helped us measure it. Another part was actively changing what was there.

## 11 · A name for the small bump

So what made that small bump? The answer the team found was sulfur dioxide. One sulfur atom bonded to two oxygen atoms. Its absorption band lands right at four point zero five microns. Include the reactions driven by starlight, and the models can make enough of this gas high in the atmosphere to account for the feature. Take those reactions away and the expected amount there drops dramatically. There’s an important distinction in that result. The telescope measured the missing infrared light. The pathway that produced the gas came from testing chemistry against that measurement. Nobody watched an individual molecule being assembled seven hundred light years away.

## 12 · Ask the molecule twice

A good identification gives you somewhere else to look. A molecule can vibrate in more than one way. If sulfur dioxide really was responsible, it should absorb at other infrared wavelengths too. Those other bands were outside the reach of the first observation. So a team looked with Webb’s mid-infrared instrument, which measures longer waves. In the new spectrum they found absorption near seven point seven and eight point five microns, where sulfur dioxide has two more bands. Think how different that is from taking the original data and drawing the same line more carefully. Here the molecule had to turn up in two new places. It did.

## 22 · An independent test

The wavelengths are known from laboratory spectra. So, um, before the new observation, there’s already a prediction on the table.

## 20 · What a micron buys you

It’s worth pausing over what it means to change instruments here. Visible light ranges from roughly four tenths to seven tenths of a micron. The first little bump lay well past red, and the follow-up went nearly twice as far out in wavelength again. Your eye would see none of these differences. In our diagrams the colors are a key, so you can follow one band through the experiment. Webb actually recorded counts on a detector. Those counts could have come back at the ordinary level in the new bands. One reason the longer-wavelength experiment mattered was precisely that the outcome was still open when the telescope was pointed.

## 13 · Where the measurement ends

Does identifying the gas tell us exactly how much there is? That’s a harder question. The depth of a band depends on how many absorbing molecules the light meets along its way. Change the temperature and gas can occupy a different range of heights. Put a cloud high in the atmosphere and starlight can lose access to the layers below it. Several combinations can leave a similar mark over a short patch of spectrum. That’s why observing more bands is so valuable. Each has to be explained by the same atmosphere. Even then, the result is a range of plausible amounts, tied to assumptions you can go back and test.

## 14 · Two edges of one world

There’s a second limit built into our original picture. We treated the ring of air as one place. But during the first moments of a transit, one edge is already in front of the star while the opposite edge is still outside it. The order reverses as the planet leaves. Keep the timing information and, with precise enough measurements, those two edges can begin to tell different stories. A later Webb analysis of Wasp thirty-nine b found evidence that its evening side is warmer than its morning side along this boundary. The same dip that told us about the gas also contains a clue to how conditions change around the planet.

## 15 · Reading an unseen sky

Go back to the point of light before the planet crossed it. With a single count you could have learned the size of a shadow. By keeping the colors separate, astronomers could trace a thin ring of gas. By paying attention to the small peak beside the obvious one, they could test what ultraviolet light does to sulfur on a world that circles its star in four days. And by going back for longer wavelengths, they gave that explanation a chance to fail. The next time you see a graph of an alien sky, look between the big peaks. Sometimes the smallest mark is the one that tells you what’s happening there.
