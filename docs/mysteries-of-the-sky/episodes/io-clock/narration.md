# The Moon That Ran Late

## 01 · A clock in the dark

In sixteen seventy-six, an astronomer predicted that a moon of Jupiter would come out of eclipse ten minutes late. The moon came back about ten minutes after the expected time. What had changed was where we were watching from.

## 02 · The tick

This is Io. Sunlight makes it visible. Once per orbit, Io passes into Jupiter’s shadow and goes dark. When Io clears the shadow, sunlight reaches it again. That reappearance gives us a tick. The next tick comes roughly forty-two and a half hours later at Jupiter.

## 03 · The journey to Earth

We only see the tick when the light reaches Earth. Over several months, Earth can move farther from Jupiter. Replay the same moment from each position. At the nearer position, the flash reaches the telescope first. At the farther position, the light is still on its way. The moon has already reappeared in both views.

## 04 · The extra time adds up

Now follow a series of eclipses as Earth moves away. Each signal has a little farther to go than the one before it. The delay between two ticks is small. Count many ticks and the accumulated shift becomes obvious. Ole Rømer used that trend to predict the late reappearance.

## 05 · The night of the prediction

On November ninth, the Paris Observatory saw Io at five thirty-five and forty-five seconds in the evening. That was about ten minutes later than expected from the August observations. Jupiter’s moon gave astronomers a clock. Their moving vantage point let them measure the travel time of light.
