# The Light Oxygen Makes

Long-form script, about 2,300 words. At the measured pace of the WASP-39 b episode, this is about 12–13 minutes. Titan narrates every chapter except 21–23, which are Professor Rowan Ellery's interview inserts.

## 01 · Why green?

Why is the brightest aurora green? Not blue like the daytime sky, and not white like the Moon. From the International Space Station, astronauts sometimes look down on auroral curtains glowing along Earth’s edge. That green light is concentrated at one wavelength, near five hundred and fifty-seven point seven nanometers. Pin that number down and you have the start of the answer. Identifying the atom behind it took almost sixty years. The color comes from a single kind of atom, a particular way it stores energy, and the height at which it happens to be floating. Welcome to Mysteries of the Sky. This is the story of the light oxygen makes.

## 02 · A line without a name

In eighteen sixty-seven, the Swedish physicist Anders Ångström turned a spectroscope on the northern lights. A spectroscope spreads light out by wavelength, the same way a prism spreads sunlight into a rainbow. Light from a hot solid fills in the whole rainbow. Light from a thin glowing gas does something different. It shows up as a few bright, narrow lines, and each kind of atom has its own pattern of lines, like a barcode. The aurora's brightest line sat in the green. And it matched nothing. Not hydrogen, not helium, not any gas that chemists could make glow in a laboratory tube. For decades, some people wondered whether the upper atmosphere contained an element nobody had found yet.

## 03 · The laboratory answer

The answer came in nineteen twenty-five, from John McLennan and Gordon Shrum in Toronto. They filled a discharge tube mostly with helium and added a small amount of oxygen, then passed an electric current through it. Under those conditions the mysterious green line appeared. They had made it with oxygen. That raised a sharper question. Oxygen had been studied in laboratories for a long time. Why had nobody seen this line before? The reason turned out to be the most important part of the whole story. In most laboratory discharges, collisions snuff out that glow before it can appear.

## 04 · Where the electrons come from

To see why, follow the energy that makes the aurora. It starts with the Sun, which blows a thin stream of charged particles outward in every direction. Earth's magnetic field holds most of that stream off, and stretches the field on the night side into a long tail. Energy builds up in that tail. When it's released, electrons already in Earth's magnetic surroundings are accelerated and sent racing along field lines toward the poles. They end up hitting the atmosphere in a ring around each magnetic pole. That ring is called the auroral oval. Seen from space, it looks like a crown of light over the high latitudes.

## 14 · When the oval grows

The oval isn't fixed. When fast solar wind or a magnetized cloud from the Sun reaches Earth, it can feed extra energy into the tail and push the oval toward the equator. In May twenty twenty-four, during the strongest geomagnetic storm in about two decades, people saw aurora from places that almost never get it, far south of the usual high latitudes. Many of those photographs were red and pink rather than green. Height and electron energy both help explain that red. A viewer far from the oval is looking at it sideways, over the curve of the Earth. The tall red glow, hundreds of kilometers up, can rise above the horizon when the green layer below it is still hidden. So distance from the aurora can change which color you see first.

## 05 · One collision

Now follow one of those electrons down a field line. It typically carries a few thousand electron volts of energy, far more than any single atom needs. As it plunges into the upper atmosphere, it hits atoms and molecules over a range of heights. Green aurora often peaks just above a hundred kilometers. Most of that air is nitrogen and oxygen. At these heights, a good share of the oxygen exists as single atoms, split apart by ultraviolet sunlight. When the electron, or one of the slower electrons it knocks loose, strikes an oxygen atom, it can lift one of the atom's electrons into a higher energy state. The atom is now holding a little extra energy. Sooner or later, it has to give it back.

## 06 · A fixed amount of energy

An atom can't hold just any amount of energy. Its states sit on fixed levels, like rungs on a ladder. When it drops from one rung to a lower one, it releases the difference as a single photon. A bigger drop makes a bluer photon. A smaller drop makes a redder one. For the oxygen state that makes auroral green, the drop is about two point two electron volts. That puts the green line at about five hundred and fifty-seven point seven nanometers. The same line is there over Norway, over Canada, and in the light that reaches orbit. The color is set by the atom, not by how fast the incoming electron was moving.

## 15 · Reading the line

How do we know the wavelength so precisely? Point a spectrograph at a green curtain and the light spreads out along a detector. One narrow green stripe stands out. Measure where that stripe falls and you have the wavelength. Then a short calculation turns it into energy. The energy of a photon equals Planck's constant times the speed of light, divided by the wavelength. Put in five hundred and fifty-seven point seven nanometers and you get about two point two two electron volts. That's the size of the drop inside the atom, to two decimal places. Nothing about the curtain's brightness or shape changes that number. The light carries a precise record of the step it came from.

## 07 · The slow atom

Here's the strange part. Many excited atoms give their energy back almost immediately, in about a hundred-millionth of a second. This green-emitting oxygen state waits. On average, it holds its energy for nearly a second before releasing the photon. Physicists call the transition forbidden. That doesn't mean it can't happen. It means the usual, fast way of emitting light isn't allowed by the rules of quantum mechanics. The atom has to use a much less likely route, and that takes time. A second sounds short. For an atom, it's an eternity.

## 08 · Why the lab missed it

Think about what can happen in that second. In a laboratory tube, or in the air around you, the gas is crowded. A typical atom is bumped by its neighbors billions of times every second. If an excited oxygen atom gets hit before it emits, the collision can carry the energy away as motion instead of light. Physicists call that quenching. In dense gas, the green state is almost always quenched first. McLennan and Shrum saw the line once they filled their oxygen discharge with a lot of helium. In the open upper atmosphere, the air is so thin that an oxygen atom can wait out its second undisturbed. The sky over the poles is doing something a normal laboratory can't easily copy.

## 09 · Height sets the color

That waiting time doesn't just explain the green. It explains where the green appears. Below about a hundred kilometers, collisions become frequent enough that the green finds it much harder to shine. So the bottom of a green curtain often has a sharp edge. Higher up, oxygen has a second slow state, one level lower. It makes red light, at six hundred and thirty nanometers, and it waits far longer, closer to two minutes. Only very thin air leaves an atom alone that long. So the red glow lives at the top, often above about two hundred kilometers. The tall aurora becomes a layered column, red at the top, green in the middle, all from the same element. The atom's patience decides the height.

## 10 · The other colors

Nitrogen adds the rest of the palette. Nitrogen molecules hit by fast electrons can lose an electron themselves, and the resulting ions glow in violet and blue. Neutral nitrogen, excited low down where the most energetic electrons reach, can give a pink or crimson fringe at the bottom of a curtain. Those emissions are fast, so they appear wherever the collisions happen. Our eyes add one more twist. In very dim light, our eyes pick up blue-green light best and barely distinguish color at all. A faint aurora that a camera records as red and purple can look grey or green to a person standing under it.

## 11 · Why curtains

So why do we see curtains, and not a smooth glow? The electrons don't arrive evenly. They're channeled down in thin sheets that follow the magnetic field, sometimes only a few hundred meters thick but thousands of kilometers long. Each sheet lights up some of the oxygen along its path. When you look along a sheet, you see through a great depth of glowing gas at once, and it looks bright and sharp. Look across it, and it's faint. As the sheets shift and fold, the curtains ripple. And because the green comes after a pause of almost a second, that wait blurs the fastest flickers. They show up more clearly in the quicker emissions.

## 12 · Seen from orbit

From the space station, about four hundred kilometers up, astronauts look down on the same layers from above. The green band sits low, near the hundred-kilometer level, close to where the atmosphere starts to quench it. When red is visible, it usually reaches higher, fading toward space. Every bit of that green light is the same tiny drop on the same atomic ladder, repeated by countless atoms at once.

## 17 · The sky is never fully dark

Even on a night without any aurora, oxygen keeps glowing. During the day, ultraviolet sunlight splits oxygen molecules high in the atmosphere. At night, some of those free atoms find each other again. The energy released by that chemistry can leave an oxygen atom in the same slow green state. This faint light is called airglow. From the ground it's faint and easy to miss. From orbit, cameras can trace it as a thin green line around the planet, about ninety to a hundred kilometers up. It's the same transition as the aurora. Only the source of the energy is different: chemistry instead of a stream of electrons.

## 18 · Green on Mars

If the green depends on oxygen atoms and thin air, it doesn't have to be an Earth thing. Mars has very little oxygen gas, but its air is mostly carbon dioxide, and sunlight splits some of those molecules high above the surface, freeing oxygen atoms. In March twenty twenty-four, after a burst of solar particles reached Mars, the Perseverance rover pointed its cameras at the night sky and its spectrometer at the glow. It recorded the same green line, five hundred and fifty-seven point seven nanometers, from the surface of another planet. It was faint, probably too faint to see by eye. But it was the same oxygen, making the same step, on another world.

## 13 · Answer

So, why is the brightest aurora green? Because the electrons that the magnetic field guides down near the poles land, again and again, at around a hundred kilometers. At that height, a good share of the oxygen is single atoms. A collision can leave an atom in a state that waits almost a second and then gives up exactly two point two electron volts. There's enough oxygen to excite, and the air is thin enough for it to glow. Go lower and collisions steal the light. Higher up, fewer collisions and gentler electrons favor the slower red. The green line that once looked like a new element was ordinary oxygen, behaving in a way it rarely gets the room to behave on the ground. The next time you see a curtain of green, you're looking at an atom's slowest kind of patience, spread across a thousand kilometers of sky.
