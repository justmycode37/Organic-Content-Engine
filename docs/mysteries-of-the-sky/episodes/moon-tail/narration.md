# The Moon Has a Tail

## 01 · A light in the wrong place

Why did a patch of yellow light appear on the opposite side of the sky from the Moon? In November nineteen ninety-eight, a camera in west Texas had been watching the night after the Leonid meteor shower. The Moon was new. It was near the Sun in the sky, on the daylight side of Earth. And yet there was a patch on the night side that looked as if it belonged to the Moon. It came back the next night, much brighter. Then it faded. What the camera saw was the end of a trail.

## 02 · Three nights in Texas

The team was looking for sodium in Earth's upper atmosphere. Meteors can leave streaks of it high above the ground, and the camera could pick out its yellow light. On the night of the shower, the scientists saw some of those streaks. The patch that emerged over the next three nights was different. It was broad, about three degrees across. And it was faint. The brightest measurement was ninety rayleighs, a unit for how much light comes from a column of glowing gas. In a wide-angle image, it was one diffuse patch among a great many stars.

## 03 · An unlikely place to look

Put the Sun at one end of the picture and the Earth at the other. Then put the Moon between them. That is the arrangement at new Moon, seen from above the orbit. From the daylight side of Earth, you would look toward the Moon and the Sun in almost the same direction. Now walk to the night side and look straight away from the Sun. That is approximately where the glow appeared. The camera was facing away from the apparent position of the Moon. Before we can explain that, we need to know what the camera was actually picking up.

Rowan

The camera wasn't telling us what shape the gas had in space. It was adding up the light coming from each direction. A compact patch could be a small cloud, or it could be a long column seen almost end-on. Those can look very similar in a photograph.

## 04 · Why yellow

Imagine spreading sunlight out into a band of colors. Most of those wavelengths will pass an isolated sodium atom with little effect. But around five hundred and eighty-nine nanometers, there are two closely spaced wavelengths that sodium scatters especially well. An atom absorbs a photon, briefly changes its energy, then emits light as it drops back. That is how very thin gas can become visible from far away. Use a filter that admits light near those wavelengths, and the camera has a much better chance of seeing sodium against the rest of the sky.

## 05 · Atoms from a world of rock

The Moon has no air like the air at Earth's surface. It does have an exosphere. That is a scattering of atoms so thin that they mostly fly past each other without colliding. Sodium is one of those atoms, and some of it comes from the Moon's soil. A grain of dust hitting at high speed can vaporize a little of that soil. Sunlight can release atoms too, and particles from the solar wind can knock them loose. Individual atoms leave in different directions and at different speeds. Some fall back. Some escape.

## 06 · A push made of light

Once an atom escapes, sunlight does more than help us see it. Imagine a photon coming in from the left. It carries a tiny amount of momentum. When a sodium atom absorbs it, that momentum nudges the atom away from the Sun. The atom can send the next photon out in any of many directions. Those recoil directions vary from one event to the next. But the incoming push has the same bias every time. Over many encounters, sunlight drives escaping sodium away from the Sun. A thin haze near the Moon becomes a very long tail.

## 07 · A tail changes its address

Now let the Moon go around Earth. The tail always extends generally away from the Sun. So it moves across different parts of space as the Moon goes round. At first quarter, Earth sits off to one side of the tail. Near full Moon, the Moon is farther from the Sun than Earth is, and its tail extends even farther out. Near new Moon, Earth is on the other side of the Moon from the Sun. It can lie in the very region the tail passes through. That is when the camera has a chance to look along the tail.

## 08 · Earth bends the stream

There is one more effect. Watch a sodium atom that would otherwise pass well above Earth. Earth's gravity pulls the atom toward the planet as it flies by. By the time it leaves, its path has turned a little inward. Another atom passing below Earth turns upward. Put a whole stream of those paths together and they begin to converge on the far side. The result is a denser core beyond Earth. The planet is acting as a gravitational lens for moving atoms. These are paths of matter, and the tighter beam does not sit on Earth's surface.

## 09 · The camera turns around

Follow the beam from the Moon, around Earth, and farther out into the night. Now turn the view so we are standing on Earth, looking along it. A camera adds light from the sodium it sees along that line of sight. Look a little to one side and the sightline leaves the dense core sooner. Look along it and the camera adds light over a much greater distance. That is how a stream hundreds of thousands of kilometers long can appear as one faint spot on the sky. We are seeing it almost from the inside.

## 10 · A test between two continents

If the glow were a quirk of one camera, it needn't show up anywhere else. A team in Japan had an all-sky camera tuned for sodium too. They found a similar feature during the same Leonid period, from a different observing site. And when the researchers checked images from August, they found the kind of feature they had missed before. The Moon did not need a spectacular meteor shower to make a tail. The unusual November event helped reveal a phenomenon that can show up around new Moon in other months.

## 18 · Two ways to get brighter

Rowan

A brighter patch is tempting. You might think it means the Moon released more sodium. But if Earth moves closer to the center of the stream, we can see a brighter patch even if the Moon releases the same amount. That is why the viewing geometry has to come first.

## 11 · What the timing tells you

The original three-night measurements peaked at new Moon, two nights after the meteor shower's maximum. Both timing clues matter. The atoms take time to travel from the Moon to Earth's vicinity. And the geometry can make the same stream easier or harder to see. A much longer study used observations from Argentina spanning fourteen years. It found that the Sun, Moon and Earth arrangement was the dominant influence on the patch's brightness. After taking geometry into account, the researchers also found a relationship with ordinary sporadic meteors. That is evidence that impacts help supply the tail, even when no named shower is in progress.

## 12 · Following the atoms in motion

Brightness is only one thing an instrument can measure. Later observations examined the wavelength of the sodium light closely enough to see a Doppler shift. When the gas recedes, the line shifts a little toward longer wavelengths. One team measured an average recession speed of twelve point four kilometers per second on three nights close to new Moon. It was relative to Earth, along their line of sight. The result fits the idea that these are atoms streaming away and gaining speed under sunlight. What had started as a patch of light became a measurement of motion.

## 13 · The light in the wrong place

Go back to the camera in Texas. Its filter was picking out sodium, its view was directed away from the Sun, and Earth itself was in the way of the stream. All three facts now fit together. Sodium escaped from the Moon. Sunlight drove it outward. Earth's gravity brought some of its paths closer together. The camera was looking down that long, faint beam. The Moon was on the other side of the sky, but some of the Moon's atoms were right out in front of the camera.
